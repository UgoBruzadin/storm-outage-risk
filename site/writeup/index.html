⚡ Storm Outage Risk

When a storm moves through, which counties will lose power? This model ranks every county by how likely the storm is to cause outages there, which tells utilities and emergency managers where repair crews will be needed.

Summary

The problem. Before severe weather, utilities send repair crews to wait near where they expect damage. Forecasts say when a storm will arrive, but not where it will do the most damage. The same storm can cut power to 2% of one county and 40% of the next.

What I built. A model that estimates, for every county and every 6-hour period, the chance that a significant share of customers lose power. It combines public weather data (wind, gusts, rain, ice, storm instability) with what each place is like (size, tree cover, housing age, density) and whether it already had outages in the past day.

How well it works. I tested it on 2025 (1 January to 31 December), a year it never trained on. With an alert level fixed on 2024, it flagged about 1% of county-periods; those were 25× more likely than average to have a major new outage, and they caught about a quarter of all of them. Weather does most of the work; local factors add a little. The model beats National Weather Service warnings and Storm Prediction Center outlooks used alone, and adding those forecasts to the model improves it by another 15%.

What it would be worth. If a utility had sent repair crews ahead of storms wherever the model pointed in 2025, it would have avoided an estimated $4–39 million in outage costs, depending on assumptions about crews. Acting on the official forecasts the same way would mostly have lost money.

One caveat. The model currently sees the weather as it actually happened, not as it was forecast, so these scores are the best case for planning ahead. Feeding it forecasts made hours in advance is the next step.

Where the effort went. Mostly into making the numbers trustworthy. Public data hides traps: gaps that look like "no outages", and one county's outages reported under its neighbour's code. There's also a model that looks great just by predicting outages that are already underway. Each trap is written up below, with the code that fixes it.

How it started

This began as a SQL exercise: hourly weather from St. Louis Lambert airport joined to EAGLE-I outage counts, to see whether bad weather lined up with outages in St. Louis. It did, and it raised a better question. When a storm crosses the state, which counties go dark, and why does the same storm take out 2% of one county and 40% of the next? My guess was trees, old dense canopy over overhead lines, and old housing. Testing that guess honestly turned out to be the hard part.

This project predicts, for every county and every 6-hour block, whether at least 1%, 5% or 10% of customers lose power, from storm ingredients (wind, gusts, wind shear, instability, rain, ice) and from what each place is like: tree canopy, housing age, density, and its own outage history. Partway through I found a related project, weathernext-outage-forecasting, forecasts county outages from weather forecasts and evaluates on random days. This one differs where it matters for an honest answer: place features, splits by time and by county, and scoring new outages separately from ongoing ones.

What I found

Outages last: a county that is out now will likely still be out in six hours, and a model can look accurate by learning only that. The useful part is the new outage, one that starts after six calm hours, so every result below is scored on new outages only.

Scores are PR-AUC: how well the model puts real outages ahead of false alarms. Random guessing scores the outage rate itself (0.005 for 5% outages), so the final model's 0.106 is about 20× better than chance.

XGBoost, 800 trees of depth 4. Settings were chosen on 2024 (trained on 2020–2023) and checked over five random seeds; 2025 was scored once.

  • Weather predicts new outages 6–24× better than chance, and the advantage grows with outage size.
  • More years of storms helped more than any feature. Training on 2020–2024 instead of 2024 alone lifted every score by 17–28% on the same 2025 test.
  • Place helps for known counties, not new ones. County size, housing and canopy add 25–34% on the next year but almost nothing on unseen counties: the model learns which counties are fragile more than why.
  • On new outages the model is a weather model. Its top features are surface wind, the block's minimum temperature (ice and cold-season storms), county size and wind × rain. Scored on every period instead, the previous period's outage is the top feature, seven times any weather or place feature: it is predicting outages already underway.
  • Large outages are the most predictable relative to their rarity. Of the 1% of calm county-periods the model ranks riskiest, about 1 in 7 become a ≥ 5% outage (26× the average), and together they catch 26% of all new ≥ 5% outages. Ranking a year after the fact isn't something a live system can do, so the alert level is also fixed in advance, as the top 1% of a 2024 validation run: on 2025 it flags 1.1% of calm county-periods, 13% of them become a ≥ 5% outage (25×), and they catch 27%.
  • Tuning barely matters. The top ten settings on 2024 sit within ±1.2% of each other, the same as the spread across random seeds. Up-weighting rare events hurt.
  • Tree canopy is why I compare across models and seeds. With LightGBM on one year of data, removing canopy cost 12–22% of PR-AUC; with XGBoost the effect shrank or reversed. With five years and five seeds it settled: removing canopy lowers the score with every seed in three of four settings, by 2–3%. A small, real effect, and still an association, not a cause.
  • Two ideas didn't make it. Rain in the days before a storm (wet ground uproots trees) helped in some settings and hurt in others across seeds, and each county's outage history over the previous three years added nothing on the validation year. Both are left out of the final model.

How it compares

A model is only worth something next to what people already use. Three comparisons, each scored the same way as everything else on this page: new outages of at least 5% of customers, PR-AUC, same data.

Against the official forecasts

A utility watching a storm already has NWS warnings (severe thunderstorm, tornado, high wind, winter) and SPC's Day 1 convective outlook. Each becomes a baseline: the historical outage rate for every combination of warnings in force, or for every outlook risk level, learned on the training year.

The two sides know different things, so the comparison is about information, not a fair race. The official forecasts are taken as they stood when the block started: the latest outlook (issued 0.6–10 hours earlier) and the warnings already in force. The model's HRRR weather describes the block itself, close to what actually happened. It knows the storm arrived; the forecasts had to anticipate it.

  • The model scores 2.4× the official forecasts on the next year and 2.2× on unseen counties, at every outage size.
  • Official forecasts still add to the model. Given the warnings and outlook as inputs, it gains 15% on the next year and 16% on unseen counties. Forecasters know things a single HRRR point per county misses, such as a high-wind or ice-storm warning issued hours ahead. Trained on 2024 alone the gain was 29%: with more years of storms, the model learns more of it on its own.
  • Counting warnings honestly matters. A first version that counted any warning touching the block made warnings look nearly twice as good. Of 1,445 new ≥ 5% outages in 2025, 335 had a severe thunderstorm or tornado warning somewhere in the block but only 65 had one in force at its start; the rest were issued as the storm, and often the outage, arrived.
  • The fair race is still open. Matching the outlook's lead time means feeding the model weather forecasts made hours before the block. That is the next step on the roadmap.

Against weathernext-outage-forecasting's setup

The related project publishes no scores, so its setup is reproduced here on this project's data, from its code: weather features only (mapped to the closest HRRR fields), a boosted tree and a logistic regression with balanced class weights, the same ≥ 5% label, and an 80/20 split by hash of county + date.

  • 1.5–2× their score on every split. Place and recent-outage features, and shallower trees without class weights, make the difference; the gap is widest on the next year.
  • Their headline metrics hide it. On random days their setup reports ROC-AUC 0.77 and, at a 0.5 cut-off, 60% recall, which reads well, but only 2% of those alerts are real (F1 0.04). Class weighting buys recall by flooding the alert list.
  • Not their model. They use Google's WeatherNext forecasts at 24–48 h lead in Alabama; this reproduces the method, not their inputs or region.

Against published research

The closest operational work is the University of Connecticut and Eversource outage prediction model, running since 2015: machine-learning ensembles over high-resolution weather forecasts, vegetation, land cover and infrastructure, predicting outage counts up to three days ahead (Cerrai et al., 2019, IEEE Access; Watson et al., 2020, The Journal of Engineering), extended to communities across the Eastern U.S. (Taylor et al., 2023, Energy Reports). Earlier work modeled hurricane outages with generalized additive models and random forests (Han, Guikema & Quiring, 2009, Risk Analysis, and later work by Nateghi and colleagues). Those studies predict outage counts for a single utility's territory, mostly for named storms, with utility data this project doesn't have, so their scores aren't comparable. What this project adds is a public-data, multi-utility, county-level version for everyday convective and winter storms, evaluated on a future year and on new outages only.

What it would be worth

A better score only matters if it changes a decision. The decision here: before a storm, a utility can send repair crews to wait in a county. Staging costs money every time. It pays off only if that county then loses power, because restoration starts sooner and customers are out for less time. So the question is: if a utility had staged crews wherever each forecast pointed in 2025, how much would it have saved?

Without dollar figures

Forecasters value forecasts with the cost-loss model. Staging costs C; an outage you weren't ready for costs L more than one you were. A forecast is worth acting on when its probability exceeds C/L. Its relative value is the share of a perfect forecast's savings it captures, over the better of "never stage" and "always stage": 1 is perfect, 0 is no better than either. The model reaches 0.52, against 0.34 for warnings and outlook together, 0.31 for the outlook and 0.09 for warnings, and it is ahead at every cost-loss ratio (left panel). This part needs no assumptions about money.

  • The estimate: $4–39 million saved in 2025 across Missouri and Illinois, where acting on the official forecasts would mostly have lost money. The new outages of 2025 cost customers an estimated $1.1 billion; the model's alerts would have avoided 0.3–3% of that.
  • The official forecasts lose money because they point at too many places. In the 25%, $5k scenario the model stages crews 2,383 times; warnings and outlook together would stage them 11,697 times and NWS warnings 17,502 times, mostly in counties that kept their power.
  • Checked generously. The official forecasts were fitted only on periods with no outage under way, so their probabilities answer the same question as the model's. Given each forecast's best possible cutoff in hindsight, chosen on 2025 itself, the model still saves about three times as much ($40.8M against $13.7M for warnings and outlook, in the 25%, $5k scenario). The model's own rule comes within 10% of its best cutoff, so its probabilities can be used as they are.
  • Businesses carry the value. Counting only what outages cost households, staging never pays, even with a perfect forecast: a home loses about $14 in an 8-hour outage. Almost all the avoided cost is businesses', so the estimate moves with the customer mix.
  • What the estimate is not. It counts customers' losses, not the utility's own costs. It covers one year, and like every score here it is a best case, because the model sees each period's weather as it happened. With forecast weather it would be lower.

Pitfalls, with code

Each of these produced a plausible-looking wrong answer before it was caught. The fixes live in the dbt models and Python modules named on each card, and most are guarded by a test.

Data · dbt/models/intermediate/int_eaglei__blocks_6h.sql

1. A missing row is not a zero

EAGLE-I only writes a row for a county that has customers out. No row can mean "no outages", or it can mean the collector was down. In one county the two look identical. Across the whole country they don't: somewhere in the U.S. someone is always without power, so a block with few national snapshots means the system was down.

-- national "heartbeat": how many 15-min snapshots exist in each 6-hour block
SELECT {{ block_6h('snapshot_time') }} AS block_start, count(*) AS n_snapshots
FROM (SELECT DISTINCT snapshot_time FROM {{ ref('stg_eaglei__outages') }})
GROUP BY 1

-- then, per county and block (mart_study__region_6h.sql)
CASE WHEN NOT b.eaglei_ok        THEN NULL   -- system down: unknown
     WHEN o.region_fips IS NULL  THEN 0      -- system up, no row: a real zero
     ELSE o.peak_out END AS peak_out

Without this, outage-free blocks and blind blocks get the same label, and the model is trained on holes in the data.

Data · dbt/models/intermediate/int_study__outages_6h.sql

2. Duplicate snapshots double the outage

In early September 2025 every snapshot was recorded twice. Summing counties into regions directly would double every outage in those weeks. The fix is to collapse to one value per county per 15-minute slot first, and only then add counties together.

WITH per_county_slot AS (
    SELECT fips_code, snapshot_time, max(customers_out) AS customers_out  -- keep one copy
    FROM {{ ref('stg_eaglei__outages') }}
    GROUP BY ALL
)
SELECT {{ region_fips('fips_code') }} AS region_fips, snapshot_time,
       sum(customers_out) AS customers_out                               -- now it's safe to add
FROM per_county_slot
GROUP BY ALL
Data · dbt/macros/region_fips.sql

3. One county filed under its neighbour's code

From mid-2020 to 2024, St. Louis County's outages were reported under St. Louis City's FIPS code. Taken at face value, the County (about 594,000 customers) had zero customers out for three straight years, while the City's worst outage grew from 5,533 in 2019 to 83,685 in 2023, nearly 80% of its 107,000 customers. Both are modeled as one region, through a macro used everywhere a county code enters the pipeline.

-- largest outage per year, EAGLE-I as published

SQL · dbt/models/marts/mart_study__region_6h.sql

4. least(100, NULL) is 100

The label caps percent-out at 100. In DuckDB, least() skips NULLs, so a blank outage count came back as "100% of customers out", a fake catastrophe in every block with a missing count.

-- wrong: least(100.0, 100.0 * NULL / customers) = 100
CASE WHEN g.peak_out IS NULL OR g.peak_out > 1.5 * g.customers THEN NULL
     ELSE least(100.0, 100.0 * g.peak_out / g.customers) END AS peak_pct_out

A dbt test (assert_pct_null_when_peak_null) fails the build if it ever comes back.

Geo · ingest/fetch_tree_canopy.py

5. The raster's "no data" value was a real value

The tree-canopy GeoTIFF tags 0 as "no data", but 0 means "no trees". rasterio.mask honours the tag, so every treeless pixel was dropped and farm counties came out about 45% forest instead of about 2%. The fix reads raw pixels, draws the county shape itself, and masks the real no-data codes (254/255).

pixels = src.read(1)
inside = geometry_mask([geometry], out_shape=pixels.shape, transform=src.transform, invert=True)
values = pixels[inside]
values = values[values <= 100]           # 254/255 = no data; 0 stays: it means no trees
Modeling · ml/data.py

6. A model that predicts outages already underway

Outages last for hours, so the previous block's outage is a superb predictor of this one. With it, PR-AUC on all blocks jumps to 0.42. That is a real forecast, of whether an outage will continue, but it answers a different question from this project's: which calm county goes dark next. Mixing the two inflates the score fourfold and hides how well the model does on the question that matters for staging crews. Scored only on new outages (blocks whose previous six hours were calm), the same model scores 0.106.

df["is_onset"] = df["prev_block_pct_out"].notna() & (df["prev_block_pct_out"] < threshold_pct)

7. The hardest test changed when the data did

Splitting region-days at random puts neighbouring counties hit by the same storm on both sides, which I expected to flatter the model most. It didn't. Trained on 2024 alone, the next year was the hard test: 0.056 for weather only, against 0.083 on unseen counties. With five training years that gap closed, and counties the model has never seen became the hardest test. Year-to-year differences were mostly a shortage of storms; a new place stays genuinely hard. Every result is reported on both.

if kind == "time":                               # train 2020–2024, test 2025
    assert max(train_years) < min(test_years)

Evaluation · ml/metrics.py

8. Ties in "top 1%" flatter the baseline

The historical-average baseline gives every block in a county-month the same score. Taking "everything at or above the 99th percentile" then flags far more than 1% of blocks, and its recall looks better than it is. Alerts are now exactly the top k, with ties broken at random (seeded).

k = max(1, int(round(top * len(p))))
order = np.lexsort((np.random.default_rng(0).random(len(p)), -p))   # by score, ties random
alert = np.zeros(len(p), dtype=bool); alert[order[:k]] = True
Modeling · ml/benchmarks/seeds_ablate.yaml

9. One run's feature importance is an anecdote

With LightGBM on one year of data, removing tree canopy cost 12–22% of next-year PR-AUC, which would have confirmed my starting guess. XGBoost shrank or reversed the effect. Later, rain in the days before a storm looked like a 2–4% gain in a single run. Both were then repeated with five random seeds: canopy's effect held (lower score without it, with every seed, in three of four settings) and rain's didn't (better in some settings, worse in others). Feature decisions are now made on the seed average, against the seed-to-seed spread.

features:
  - weather+place+recent
  - weather+place+recent-canopy      # canopy removed
  - weather+place+recent+wet_soil    # antecedent rain added
models:
  - name: xgboost
    params: {..., random_state: [0, 1, 2, 3, 4]}   # every run, five times
Modeling · ml/data.py

10. Same code, same data, different answer

The same model on the same rows scored 0.1053, 0.1059 and 0.1065 in three runs. DuckDB scans tables in parallel and returns rows in a different order each time; XGBoost samples 80% of rows per tree by position, so a different order meant a slightly different model. The ~1% wobble was as large as the feature effects being judged. One line fixed it, and every reported result was rerun.

SELECT ...
FROM m
WHERE m.has_weather AND m.peak_pct_out IS NOT NULL
ORDER BY m.region_fips, m.block_start    -- a fixed order: row subsampling depends on it

How it's built

IngestPython scripts download outages, HRRR storm ingredients, storm reports, NWS warnings, SPC outlooks, tree canopy rasters and Census tables.
Transformdbt on DuckDB: staging → intermediate → marts, 47 tests, a 3.5M-row modeling table.
ModelA YAML-driven benchmark suite: baselines, logistic, XGBoost, LightGBM; three splits; a validation year; seed repeats; MLflow tracking.
ServeStreamlit dashboard on precomputed Parquet, packaged with Docker; CI on GitHub Actions.

Python, SQL, DuckDB, dbt, pixi, scikit-learn, XGBoost, LightGBM, MLflow, geopandas, rasterio, Streamlit, Plotly, Docker, GitHub Actions.

Limitations

The weather is short-range, not a 24–48 hour forecast — the HRRR archive is close to observed weather, so the scores are an upper bound for planning a day ahead, and the model sees each block's weather while the official forecasts it is compared with were issued before the block. The final test is a single year (2025); the spread across seeds shows model noise (about ±1%) but not year-to-year variation, so there are no confidence intervals yet. Each county has a single weather point. EAGLE-I coverage is patchier before 2020, which limits how far back training can go; 2018–2019 weather is downloading.
